# Chapter 2 — Literature Review: Trizna on ML for Linux Command Detection

## Feature matrix — Trizna's approach vs. this project

| Dimension | Trizna — SLP (2021) | This project |
|---|---|---|
| **Feature type** | A purpose-built **shell tokenizer** (SLP) plus classical encodings (TF-IDF / hashing). The thesis: generic NLP tokenization mis-parses shell, so a shell-aware representation is needed. | **Hybrid** — 43 engineered behavioral features (counts, ratios, entropies, MITRE binary-family flags) + character (3–5)-gram TF-IDF for the XGBoost-hybrid; a **raw character-index sequence** for the 1D-CNN. |
| **Model type** | Classical ML classifiers over SLP encodings (the paper's contribution is the *representation*, not a new model). QuasarNix (2024) later adds an ensemble of classifiers with adversarially-trained variants. | XGBoost, RandomForest, 1D-CNN, IsolationForest, and a TF-IDF+LR baseline, composed into a 3-stage cascade with LLM arbitration. |
| **Dataset type** | Real **Linux `auditd`** telemetry (single-organization logs), supervised. QuasarNix instead uses **synthetic augmentation** — >1M reverse-shell variants from ~34 templates. | **Provenance-labeled, multi-source**: D1 curated corpora (HackTricks, GTFOBins, Atomic Red Team, PayloadsAllTheThings, QuasarNix, SLP) vs. D2 operational (Cowrie honeypot + real shell histories), held at an exact 1:3 attack:benign ratio. |
| **Evaluation metric** | SLP: **F1**, reporting a lift from **0.392 → 0.874** on its security-classification task. QuasarNix: **detection rate at an industry-grade FPR = 10⁻⁵**. | **F1, ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, TPR@FPR=1%/0.1%**, plus per-source recall/FPR and near-duplicate-leakage audits. |
| **Transfer / generalization tested** | SLP: **not a cross-corpus study** (in-distribution). QuasarNix: **yes** — black-box evasion attacks and adversarially robust defenses. | **Yes, explicitly** — cross-dataset D1↔D2 transfer, leave-one-attack-source-out, and a source-separability confound probe. |

## Comparative essay

**What Trizna did.** The through-line of Trizna's command-detection work is that *representation and data realism dominate the problem*. The SLP paper (2021) argues that off-the-shelf NLP pipelines fail on Unix commands — flags, paths, quoting, and substitution break word tokenizers — and shows that a shell-aware tokenizer lifts a supervised classifier on real `auditd` telemetry from **F1 0.392 to 0.874**, a result driven entirely by the encoding rather than a heavier model. QuasarNix (2024) then attacks the *data* side of generalization directly: rather than collect more labels, it **synthesizes over a million reverse-shell variants** from ~34 threat-intel templates while preserving benign variability, evaluates at an operationally honest **FPR of 10⁻⁵** (reporting ~90% higher detection than non-augmented baselines at that threshold), and hardens the models against black-box evasion. Together they frame LotL detection as a representation-and-robustness problem, not a bigger-classifier problem.

**How our results compare.** On raw in-distribution quality our best models are in the same neighborhood as SLP's headline: the **XGBoost-hybrid reaches F1 0.876 / ROC-AUC 0.979 on Dataset 1** and **0.848 / 0.968 on Dataset 2**, and the TF-IDF+LR **baseline** is in fact *ahead* of it on both (**0.898 / 0.983**; **0.882**) — all in SLP's 0.874 F1 ballpark, though on a different, provenance-labeled corpus so the numbers are not directly commensurable. The CNN is a hair behind in-domain (**F1 0.860 / 0.838**) but, as Ch. 8 shows, transfers best. Crucially, our results echo QuasarNix's central methodological point: headline F1 flatters. At QuasarNix's kind of strict operating point, our **TPR@FPR=0.1%** for the XGBoost-hybrid is only **0.567 (D1)** and **0.526 (D2)** — i.e. even the strongest model catches only about half of attacks once false alarms are held to 1-in-1000. The gap between our ~0.97 ROC-AUC and our ~0.55 TPR@0.1%FPR is exactly the discrepancy QuasarNix's low-FPR evaluation was designed to expose.

**Baseline configuration note.** The TF-IDF + LR baseline uses character 3–5 grams with `min_df=1, max_features=None` (no vocabulary caps); removing the earlier caps (`min_df=2, max_features=40000`) was tested and changes nothing material. Its reproducible hold-out F1 is **0.8975 (D1) / 0.8824 (D2)** from `scripts/evaluate_baseline.py` → `docs/baseline_metrics.json`, which is the figure quoted above and throughout Ch. 8. That the simplest model in the project beats all five engineered-feature models on both corpora is not a footnote — it is the result Ch. 8.3 decomposes.

**What our approach adds, and trades off.** Our contribution is orthogonal to Trizna's: we make **label provenance and its confounds the object of study**. Where SLP assumes trustworthy supervised labels and QuasarNix manufactures clean synthetic ones, we take *provenance-based* labels (a command is malicious because of where it was collected) and stress-test what that buys and costs — via a source-separability probe (a classifier recovers which of 11 corpora a line came from at **0.821 accuracy vs. 0.298 chance**), leave-one-source-out recall, and an **explicit cross-dataset transfer** evaluation that neither SLP nor the assigned framing performs on Linux commands. That evaluation surfaces the finding that dominates our project: in-domain scores are inflated by corpus style. The trade-offs are real — we do **not** perform synthetic augmentation or adversarial hardening (QuasarNix's strengths), our provenance labels are noisier per-line than SLP's supervised `auditd` labels, and our data is curated/honeypot rather than a single organization's production telemetry, so our absolute numbers should be read as *relative* diagnostics.

**On the anomaly-detection framing.** Our closest analog to an unsupervised sequence model is the **IsolationForest** stage, trained on benign-only data. Its weak standalone result — **F1 0.249 (D1), 0.143 (D2)** — is consistent with the literature: for a task where malicious and benign commands overlap heavily in content (Ch. 8's dual-use false positives), pure anomaly detection under-performs supervised discrimination, which is why our cascade uses it only as a cheap first-stage bulk filter rather than the primary classifier.
